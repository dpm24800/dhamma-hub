# How to Train Your Mind to Stay Peaceful Like a Rock

*An article drawn from a Dhamma reflection*

---

Many people ask: *How can I be mentally strong?* It is a question almost everyone needs answered. The answer begins with understanding what a strong mindset actually is.

The Buddha gave a clear image of it in the *Dhammapada*:

> As a rock that is unshaken by the storm, so too the wise are unshaken by praise and blame.

Big masses of rock are not moved no matter how fast the wind blows. In the same way, wise people remain unshaken by the praise and blame they meet in life. That unshaken quality is a strong mindset — and, with practice, it can be built. Three simple tips are offered here, followed by a word on overthinking, which is one of the most common ways the mind loses its stability.

## Tip One: Maintain a Stable Mindset Through the Ups and Downs of Life

Life is like an ocean. Sometimes it is calm, quiet, and peaceful; sometimes it is stormy. We all face ups and downs. The Buddha described these as the eight worldly conditions (*loka dhamma*): praise and blame, gain and loss, fame and disgrace, pleasure and pain. These eight conditions come to everyone.

The Buddha's advice is to accept these ups and downs. Whenever people criticize you, whenever they blame you, whenever you go through tough times, mindfulness is the key. If you can be mindful of your mind, your emotions, and your feelings — and refuse to chase after them — you can remain stable. You can keep emotional balance in the most difficult situations.

This is why it is so important to practice self-awareness. And remember: you will not always receive sweet comments, sweet opinions, or sweet experiences. Life will give you tough times; life will throw lemons at you. Maintaining a stable mindset in those moments, through mindfulness and self-awareness, is the first step toward mental strength.

## Tip Two: Build Resilience Inside the Mind

Resilience is the ability to stand up and bounce back after adversity and challenge. To develop it, one thing must be clearly understood: *what is in your control, and what is not.*

If people criticize you, the words they speak about you are not in your control. What is in your control is your reaction to them. That reaction is always a choice. Ask yourself: should I become angry? Should I take this negativity on and share it back? If you can pause and ask such questions, you are no longer ready to simply carry other people's negativity.

It is also important to correct a common misunderstanding. Having a strong mindset does not mean you never fall, never feel hurt, never feel down. It means you can stand up again as quickly as possible. You fall, and then you quickly return to your normal state. That quick recovery is resilience.

To practice this, remember the boundary of control: the experiences that happen to you and the things people say — their opinions and thoughts about you — are not under your control. But everything inside your mind — your opinions, your interpretations, your reactions — is entirely under your control. Do not worry about what you cannot change; invest yourself only in what you can. You can change your choices, not other people.

Resilience also requires a problem-solving mindset. If you are easily panicked or triggered by problems, solving them becomes hard. But if you can remain calm and peaceful even while facing problems, then you can go to the root of the issue and solve it.

Too often we mistake ourselves for our problems: *we are the anger; I am this problem; why is this happening to me?* Remember that everything that happens to you is impermanent. It is not permanent; it will change. So set the problem aside and dive into its root. Ask yourself: why did this happen? What is the real reason? Where did I make mistakes? Go to the root, and you can solve it. That is how a problem-solving mindset is built.

## Tip Three: Practice Self-Love and Self-Compassion

The third and final tip is a mindset of self-love and self-compassion.

If you truly love yourself, you will not carry other people's negativity. Whatever negative thing comes toward you, you can say: "I'm not going to take this negativity with me. I want to be peaceful. I truly love myself, so I'm not ready to take your negativity — and I'm not sharing it back." That is self-love and self-compassion.

There is a simple daily practice that supports this. When you wake up early in the morning, sit on your bed and think: *no matter what happens during the day, I am going to love myself. I'm not going to take any negativity from this world.* Imagine emptying your bin and beginning the day clean.

Consider what happens to a trash bin that is never emptied: it begins to smell. But a bin that is emptied and cleaned every single day is easy to live with — easy to be peaceful with. So clear out the accumulated trash of the mind daily, through self-compassion and self-love, and build a strong mindset.

One more clarification is needed: anger is not strength. Some people believe that those who easily lose their temper, or who show toughness and rudeness, have strong minds. The opposite is true. Anger and the display of negativity are signs of weakness. If you are easily triggered by people and their reactions, that is not a strong mindset. The strong mindset is the one that is not disturbed by the ups and downs of life.

Practice these simple steps: build a stable mindset, make your mind resilient, and love yourself more than any other person in this world. In time, you can build a mind unshaken by the ups and downs of life.

## Getting Out of Your Head: A Word on Overthinking

Are you having sleepless nights because of heavy thoughts looping inside your mind? Do you imagine worst-case scenarios before doing anything new? Do you constantly worry about what other people think? If so, you may be overthinking. Here is a way out.

**Realize that overthinking does not help.** You can plan and you can analyze — but do not overdo it. Over-planning and over-analyzing do not help; they only distract you from the process itself.

**Practice mindfulness.** The Buddha taught many ways to be mindful, and one of the foremost is mindfulness of breathing — *ānāpānasati*. When you pay close attention to the inhale and the exhale, your mind instantly comes into the present moment. Instead of thousands of looping thoughts, there are just two: inhale and exhale. Naturally, peacefulness arises, and overthinking stops.

Whenever you catch yourself overthinking, take three deep breaths, in and out, and stay with your breathing for a while. Alternatively, be mindful of your body: ask yourself, *how am I sitting? what am I doing?* Recognizing such things brings the mind back to the present. And a mind in the present is a mind at peace.

There is a saying: *If you are sad, your mind is in the past. If you are anxious, your mind is in the future. But if you are peaceful and still, your mind is in the present moment.* So start living in the present moment. When you live right here, right now, you can be peaceful and break the cycle of overthinking.

**Answer the "what if" question.** Another part of overthinking is the "what if" question: what if this happened? What if everything I did was wrong? People imagine worst-case scenarios. But for every negative "what if," there is a positive one: *what if everything that I did was okay? What if everything I planned and did was really great?* That is possible too. There are always many possibilities for your plans to go well.

So do not be negative. You will not be perfect — none of us can be — but you can be the best version of yourself. Do your best, be positive, and take risks. Without risk, success in life is not possible.

## Conclusion

Be more mindful. Be more kind to yourself. Remember that overthinking and constant worry kill the peacefulness of your life. By building a stable mindset, developing resilience, practicing self-love, and coming back to the present moment, you can become like the rock of the Buddha's verse — unshaken by praise and blame, unshaken by the storm.

*Be peaceful, be mindful, get out of your head, and start doing the work you haven't done.*
