# How to Endure a Screaming Mind

*An essay drawn from a Dhamma reflection*

---

There are moments when the mind turns against us. It becomes agitated, restless, and loud — kicking, screaming, and demanding. Most people know this situation well, yet few know how to be with such a mind, how to prepare for it, or how to remain at peace without falling into despair. This essay gathers the reflections of two practitioners on enduring a screaming mind: what the practice asks of us, why the mind gets so powerful, and how to work with it honestly and gradually.

## The Ideal and the Reality

There is often a large gulf between the average practitioner's experience and the ideals of the practice. The ideal, as the suttas describe it, is patience: whatever state the mind is in, we are not to reject it but to be patient with it. Even when the mind is experiencing grief, despondency, or violent agitation, we are not supposed to solve or escape it. Escaping may bring relief, but the underlying problem — that the mind is affecting us in the first place — remains untouched.

The practice, at its core, is about being patient with any state of mind, without breaking virtue through sensuality, anger, or any other escape, and simply letting it be.

Yet at the beginning, this is very hard. In modern times especially, the mind can be strongly agitated and full of strong impulses. It often shouts at us a great deal, and being patient during those times is extraordinarily difficult.

## Look at What Comes Before

The first piece of advice is perhaps counterintuitive: do not focus on the agitation itself. For the vast majority of people, long before the mind reaches the point of screaming, there have been many micro-actions and micro-impulses that one has been following — and these are what allowed the mind to get to that stage.

These are hard to see at first. It is usually only after failing many times that one begins to look more deeply and realizes: before the mind started shouting, one was already careless — delighting in idle chatter, or in the mental anticipation of a comforting meal later, and so on.

The mind genuinely cannot become agitated unless we are actively involved in it to some degree. This is where the gradual training's teaching on "danger in the slightest fault" stops being a formula and becomes a natural part of experience. Consider the person who, while calm at work, already looks forward to going home to two hours of entertainment. If asked whether they had accepted and delighted in that plan, they would say yes — not in any massively craving way, but through just a small, quiet acceptance. Those micro-acceptances prep the mind for a larger intoxication. They bait it out. By the time the mind arrives at the agitated state, the momentum is already there, and it is almost too late to do anything about it.

So the first instruction: do not look at the specific experience of agitation. Look at what came beforehand. You will probably find plenty of craving there.

## Why the Mind Has So Much Power

Giving in to the impulses of the mind is a habit. It is not something done once a day; it is done constantly. This is why the mind has so much power over us — because we gave it that power by feeding it so often. When the mind kicks and screams, it is because it is well fed, because we have let it run loosely for so long.

By default, without any particular point where things went wrong, we already accept the mind at face value. We are already moving in the direction of what it points at. When the mind wants sweets, we want sweets. We don't see a mind of greed or a central impulse; we see ourselves wanting those things. The disconnect that reflection requires is absent, because we are right in the middle of the experience. It is the center of our experience.

To endure the screaming mind, one must suddenly accept in that moment: this is just a mind full of agitation. It is fine that it is there. One does not have to obey all of its commands. But after having given in many times in many ways, this is very difficult indeed.

## Train in Advance Instead of Seeking a Quick Fix

Most people want a quick fix for the exact situations in which they feel bad and dreadful. But it is far better not to try to find a fix inside the crisis at all. Instead, train and prepare in advance. This calls for a larger focus on wakefulness at all times — even when we are supposedly calm, for even within that calm there are all sorts of subtle agitation.

When the mind does get intense — to the point where one feels at the edge and in danger of breaking precepts or giving in to distraction — one practical approach is this: do not give the mind exactly what it wants. If it is screaming for a piece of cake, give it something — but not the cake. Perhaps a single piece of candy from the supermarket. This still involves giving in to some degree, but only to some degree. The mind does not fully get what it wants, and a little of its usual power over us is redirected away. It is a way of weaning ourselves off the mind's strong grip, especially when we are not yet well trained enough to say no outright.

## Coping Mechanisms That Are Less Severe

Expecting an overnight renunciation — living in the forest like the Buddha, with no entertainment and no possessions — is unrealistic for the vast majority of people. A gradual, wise approach is needed. But it must be engaged in without justifying it to oneself, without claiming that smaller amounts of sensuality do not matter. They are still harmful at the ultimate level. Still, some people are so deep in addiction — to anger, to entertainment, to music, to video games — that overnight calm is simply impossible.

The gradual path uses coping mechanisms that are less severe and less intense. If the mind asks for ten hours of video games, go for a walk and look at flowers instead. If it asks for entertainment from dawn to dusk, cut it down. One practitioner describes listening to ten hours of music a day and simply doing less — two hours in the morning and evening — and gradually weaning off entirely. Eventually, one can make the leap where, for the things that are always imbued with craving (such as the eight precepts), one does not let the mind go to them at all, even when it shouts. Practiced this way, the mind grows stronger, and restraint becomes more natural.

There is a problem to watch for in this approach: one can begin to assume the craving lives in the specific activities being avoided. It does not. Craving lives in the mind, in passionate intentions; one can crave even a calm walk among flowers. But for the average person, starting with the most intense manifestations is the realistic way, and from there one can become more subtle.

## Gradual, Like Lifting Weights

Enduring a screaming mind is like weight training. An untrained person cannot deadlift three hundred kilos off the floor; they will hurt themselves or simply fail. But with the right approach, the right training, good sleep, good nutrition, and a long time of doing the right things, it can be done by the vast majority of people. The Buddha encouraged starting much earlier than is comfortable — beginning all this training at sixty is much harder, because so many coping mechanisms have already accumulated.

The broader frame of reference is this: most decisions are small and daily, and one is probably driven around by the mind nearly twenty-four hours a day without noticing — because the mind presents itself as *us* wanting all those things. Undoing that is daunting. It calls for being gradual, patient, and wakeful, and for assigning the right significance to the small things. A single surrender to sweets may seem like nothing, but the *direction* it sends us in is harmful. Failure rarely comes all at once; it creeps back in over time, sneaking in even when we are watchful, until we suddenly find ourselves in the middle of whatever our addiction was. That is what to look for.

A highly developed monk would sense the danger at the very first suggestion — an innocuous thought of socializing, say — knowing that accepting it sets the whole sensual domain rolling ahead. Lay people, by contrast, accept countless small things as just part of lay life, both physically and mentally, never realizing they will eventually roll toward something much bigger.

## On Failure, and the Danger of Stopping Trying

Small things matter enormously. One could even say that all the big failures we experience are built from small decisions. People tend to fail a lot before they finally succeed.

There is a dangerous mindset, common especially among naturally averse people, of stopping even trying because one knows one might fail. The correct attitude is to make peace with the gradual nature of the process: one is only truly failing when one stops trying. An honest attempt that ends in a setback is not a failure — it is a signal, perhaps, that the required degree of graduality was misjudged. So try again, be a bit more gradual, look more carefully for the even tinier things that crept in. It is a process.

The suttas confirm that appearances are not destiny. Some people appear highly developed — even capable of *jhāna* — and yet carry an unwholesome root that destines them for decline. Others appear agitated and without perspective, yet carry a wholesome root that destines them for growth. The human state is so valuable precisely because it is the most malleable. It is shocking how much one can change in even a few months.

## The Laboratory Attitude

When the mind is shouting, large perspective is impossible — that is the whole point; the mind has gripped us. For risk-averse people, another danger appears: brooding over long-term consequences until the whole practice seems unbearable — "I can't do this for my whole life." That is one of Māra's tricks, making things seem far scarier than they are.

A useful attitude in the beginning is the laboratory attitude: treat it as an experiment. When the mind screams, say to yourself: "Let's just see what happens — just this once, just this moment. I am not committing to the rest of my life." Often, even that single experiment reveals that it is not as scary as feared. Māra has many ways of making things seem terrifying, but faced directly, Māra is something of a weakling. Through such personal experience — seeing the gripped mind not tricking us, and fading away — the mind becomes more perceptive of its own abilities, and confident.

## Build Bit by Bit

This is another phrasing of the same truth: large tasks that seem frightening must be started somewhere. Some people are paralyzed by large problems, intimidated into not starting. But a house is not built in one session; one starts somewhere and keeps building. Those who honestly try — even if they fail — learn something about themselves, and that learning is progress. The mind, however, directs us to perceive honest effort as failure, because that is how it gets what it wants from us. It can be healthy, especially at first, to deliberately bring up the positive side of trying hard and failing: progress is made precisely by failing repeatedly.

When failure does come, Māra turns it into an ontological condemnation: you are a deeply bad, guilty person who will never manage anything. But those thoughts are not yours — they are the mind trying to trick you into abandoning the practice entirely, which only makes things worse over the long term. Failure at the beginning is to be expected, and positivity must be maintained, even by force if necessary. Patience is the name of the game. Perfection overnight is impossible for the modern mind; it simply is not going to happen.

Within a few months, one is often surprised at how much less the mind showers one than before. One becomes like a boulder in the middle of the ocean, with waves crashing from every side, but unstoppable — and confidence grows naturally.

## The Danger of Overconfidence

Yet this very confidence can become a trap. As practice improves, subtle things begin to appear. One realizes, "I am becoming more confident," and then begins delighting in that confidence — and that delight is how the mind reasserts itself. A few days or weeks of calm, and one grows overconfident, starts accepting smaller things, becomes complacent. Then the mind strikes — Māra has a field day — and one is back to square one. Then comes the thought: "I failed after all that time. I thought I was doing well. I am the worst person ever." Again, Māra is having a field day.

Knowing these tricks in advance — realizing that such things will happen — helps one stay above them to whatever extent possible at the beginning. And in the very long term, though it takes time, there are genuine places of safety. When the Buddha speaks of *jhāna* and concentration, he points to states where one genuinely settles — not absolute safety like *nibbāna*, but places where the same things that used to disturb us no longer affect us in the same way. It is not all difficulty and gloom; it is simply that, at first, the mind has been let wild, and unbinding oneself from its bait is difficult.

In the beginning it can feel like pushing a great boulder up a mountain, again and again, for a very long time. But there are concentrated states where that effort is no longer necessary, where the boulder rolls up the hill on its own, and the mind is simply doing the right thing. Just as, when agitated, it becomes almost impossible to conceive of ever having a calm mind, so once calm is genuinely known, it becomes impossible to conceive of returning to a mind that blindly follows every impulse.

## Inspiration from Human Example

Sources of inspiration matter. The suttas focus largely on the Dhamma as an abstract entity rather than on personal examples, and it is hard to draw inspiration from abstract perfection. Seeing "great Dhamma warriors" who seem to overcome the mind instantly can make the ordinary practitioner feel defeated.

Reading biographies of real people who went through the same struggles helps one realize that struggling is a natural part of the training — not justification, but normalcy. It is not an ontological failure to find practice difficult. This humanizing aspect gives further faith to continue, because at the beginning the capacity for gloom, self-defeat, and self-hatred is strong unless managed.

Honest autobiography is rarer than it should be. Monks on YouTube generally will not tell you how many times they failed or how many years they wasted on their own development; that is not considered monkish. Practitioners do not highlight such things, seeing little benefit in it. One speaker offers a personal example: after finishing his graduation exams, he spent sixteen hours a day playing video games for an entire year. He does not usually share this — but it gives a frame of reference: the people one listens to are not magical beings to be placed on pedestals. They have achieved things, but that does not mean you cannot achieve the same. There is enormous room for change once one honestly begins trying without excuses — in a few months, so much progress is possible, if one puts heart and soul into it.

The impulse to share the Dhamma is itself a strange thing. People who practice deeply tend to be quiet, not conceited. But for one who has experienced the same doubts, self-hatred, depression, numbness, and addictive behaviors — and who has then seen the path out of them through the Dhamma — there is a natural inspiration to share it.

## The Jewel and the Human

There is an image of the Buddha as a complete and perfect person. That image inspires and gives faith, but it can be self-defeating, because the Buddha can seem so transcendent, almost non-human. Human figures help. Biographies of Thai forest monks — even where one disagrees with their ideas — show the flow of practice and restraint. So, too, do the lives of Christian monks, who practiced the same abandonment of sensuality and hatred. Seeing the struggles of such people, their development, and how they overcame it is genuinely helpful.

## Conclusion

Enduring a screaming mind is not about finding a fix in the moment of crisis. It is about preparation: understanding that the mind's power is built on countless small surrenders, staying wakeful even in calm, and looking at what precedes the agitation rather than at the agitation itself. It is about being gradual and patient, using less severe coping mechanisms to wean oneself off the mind's grip, and accepting that honest failure is progress. It is about knowing Māra's tricks — the fear-mongering, the ontological condemnation, the overconfidence trap — and staying above them as best one can. And it is about drawing inspiration from real, struggling, human examples, which remind us that practice is difficult for everyone, that the human state is remarkably malleable, and that genuine places of safety await those who keep trying.

*The struggle is normal. Keep trying. Make peace with the process. Be gradual, be patient, be wakeful — and understand that you are only truly failing when you stop.*
